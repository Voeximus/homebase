Label Parity

Can a photo of a nutrition label go straight into the app with numbers you can trust, without trusting an AI to read it? Mostly yes, because the label already carries its own error-checking. This is the evidence, measured on 192 real products, and the exact places it stops working.

THE IDEAA misread contradicts the rest of the label

A satellite link doesn't trust every bit it receives. It sends extra bits so the receiver can catch and repair what the channel damaged. A US nutrition label does the same thing by accident of regulation. Most gram amounts are printed a second time as a percentage. Calories are fixed by the macros. Every number has to sit on a legal rounding grid. So when a camera misreads one digit, that digit usually disagrees with the others, and ordinary deterministic code can see the disagreement.

  1. A
    Calories are a function of the macros

    Macros give 4×6 + 4×22 + 9×9 = 193. The label says 190. They aren't equal, and they don't need to be: each printed number stands for a rounded range, and 190 falls inside what those ranges allow. So the check needs almost no tolerance: 191 of 192 real labels fit with none at all.

  2. B
    The %DV column is a second copy

    9 ÷ 78 (the fat Daily Value) is 11.5%, printed as 12%. If the camera reads "9g" as 99, the percentage would have to be 127%. The misread is caught, and only one repair fits: 9.

  3. C
    Numbers live on a legal grid

    Fat under 5 g prints in half grams and above 5 g in whole grams. Calories go in 5s up to 50, then in 10s. A reading of 3.7g fat or 185 calories cannot exist on a US label.

  4. D
    The weak spot: total sugars

    No percentage is printed for total sugars. Misread 9 g as 8 g and nothing else on the label disagrees. 43% of sugar misreads pass every check. This is the field a person has to look at.

  5. E
    Parts can't exceed their total

    Saturated plus trans fat can't exceed total fat, sugars can't exceed carbs, and added sugars can't exceed total sugars. Weak on its own, useful as a final net.

THE THEORIESWhat was claimed, and what survived

Each idea started as a claim. These are the verdicts, and what each one rests on: measured means run on the 192 real products; read means taken from the regulation or a source, not measured here.

  • Holds
    The energy check needs almost no fudge factor

    Measured. Treating each printed number as its rounding range, all 96 calibration labels fit with zero extra allowance. Of the 96 held-out labels, 95 did too; one printed 1.5 kcal below what its ranges allow, 1% of its 150 kcal. With a 1% margin the false-alarm rate is 0%; without it, 1 in 96. Raw gaps between printed calories and 4P + 4C + 9F ran from −15 to +9 kcal.

  • Holds
    The %DV column is the strongest single source of checking

    Measured. With a single misread, adding the %DV check took detection from 39% to 86%: a 47-point jump, the biggest single step.

  • Holds
    Two misreads are easier to catch than one

    Measured. With every check on, two simultaneous misreads were caught 99.7% of the time, against 93.2% for one. More broken numbers contradict each other in more places.

  • Fails
    The app can fix misreads on its own

    Measured. When exactly one repair fitted every check, it was the wrong repair 4.0% of the time. For "added sugars %DV" that rose to 15.5%. So it suggests, and a person taps to accept.

  • Fails
    The checks catch a wrong serving size

    Measured. A serving read ten times too large was caught 0 times in 96: every number on the label stays consistent with every other. Numbers read from the whole-package column were caught only 32–53% of the time, and only when the gram totals outgrew the serving.

  • Fails
    An AI reader can be the dependable core

    Read. Language models give different answers to the same input even at temperature 0, because the server batches requests differently each time. It can propose; it can't be the thing you rely on.

  • Partly
    Every field checks itself

    Measured. Seven fields went silent under 2% of the time, but total sugars went silent 43% of the time and protein 12%. See the field table below.

  • Not tested
    On-phone OCR is fast enough on both your phones

    Read. PP-OCRv6 tiny (June 2026) runs fully in a browser, with English and Chinese in about 6 MB. Speed on Xinyan's iPhone is unmeasured.

  • Not tested
    Agreement across three photos covers the weak fields

    Theory. It only works if the misreads are independent between shots, but glare and a small font cause the same misread every time. Correlated errors are the risk to test first.

THREE RULEBOOKSUS, EU and China labels all carry checks, just different ones

Each regulation fixes the row order, the rounding and the energy maths, so each gives the reader a grammar to follow. Only the US column was measured. The Chinese standard looks even stronger on paper: its percentage column is computed from the printed value itself, so it's an exact match rather than a range, and it covers protein and energy.

THE NEXT EXPERIMENTWhat isn't known yet, and how to find out

Everything above tests whether the label carries enough checking. None of it tests how often a real camera misreads a real label in your kitchen. That's the next number to get, and it takes an afternoon.

  1. Photograph at least 30 labels, including some Chinese ones and some awkward ones: curved bottles, glossy wrappers, small print. Type each label's true values once.
  2. Run PP-OCRv6 tiny on both phones, through the checks above. Record the raw read, the time taken and the checks' verdicts.
  3. Count silent errors after confirmation: fields saved wrong even though serving size and sugar were confirmed. Also count how many fields a person had to touch.
A proposed pass bar: no more than 1 silent error in 200 saved fields, with no more than 3 taps per label. If it clears that on both phones, build it. If it doesn't, the misreads it misses will point at exactly what to fix.

METHOD & SOURCESHow the numbers were made

What was measured, and its limits
  • The labels: 200 branded products from one USDA FoodData Central search; 192 had usable data. USDA stores per-100 g values worked out from each label, so multiplying back by serving size recovers the printed number. 99.2% of recovered values landed exactly on the legal rounding grid, which confirms the recovery is faithful.
  • No peeking: the energy check was set on 96 products and tested on the other 96. Its one adjustable allowance came out at zero on the first half; one label in the second half sat 1% outside, so the check keeps a 1% margin.
  • The misreads are simulated from a fixed list of known OCR confusions: 0↔8, 1↔7, 3↔8, 5↔6, 9↔8, a dropped decimal point, the "g" read as a 9, and a clipped digit. Real camera error rates are not measured here.
  • %DV values were rebuilt from the printed amounts. The check accepts both ways the regulation permits.
  • Not covered: foods with sugar alcohols or a lot of fiber can legitimately print fewer calories than 4/4/9 predicts. None in this sample needed that allowance, but the sample may simply not contain many.
